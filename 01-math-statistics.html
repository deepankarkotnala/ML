Module 01 · Part A Foundations

Math & Statistics Prerequisites

You do not need a maths degree. You need fluency in a small, specific set of ideas — this is the minimum set that unlocks every module after it.

🎯
Your interview goal

Be able to derive a loss function from a probability model, state what a p-value does and does not mean, and explain why regularization is a prior.

1 · What to learn

Linear algebra

  1. Vectors, dot product, and its geometric meaning — projection, similarity, angle.
  2. Matrices as linear maps; matrix multiplication as composition.
  3. The design matrix X (n × d): rows are samples, columns are features. Internalise this shape.
  4. Transpose, inverse, rank, singularity — and what “XᵀX is not invertible” means practically.
  5. Norms: L1, L2, L∞ — and their shapes as constraint regions.
  6. Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and the covariance matrix. This is PCA.
  7. Condition number — why badly scaled features make optimization slow.

Calculus

  1. Derivative as slope; partial derivative; the gradient as the direction of steepest ascent.
  2. The chain rule. This is backpropagation.
  3. Convexity, and why it guarantees a unique global minimum.
  4. Setting the gradient to zero to find an optimum. This is the normal equation.

Probability and statistics

  1. Random variables, expectation, variance, covariance, correlation.
  2. Conditional and joint probability, independence.
  3. Bayes’ theorem, and how to state it in words.
  4. Bernoulli, Binomial, Normal, Poisson, Exponential, Uniform.
  5. Central Limit Theorem and the Law of Large Numbers — what each actually claims.
  6. Maximum Likelihood Estimation. The single highest-leverage statistical concept for ML interviews.
  7. MAP estimation, and its equivalence to regularized MLE.
  8. Estimator bias, standard error, confidence intervals — and their correct interpretation.
  9. Hypothesis testing, p-values, Type I/II error, power.
  10. Bootstrapping and permutation tests — more useful in practice than most parametric tests.
  11. Simpson’s paradox, selection bias, survivorship bias, confounding.

2 · Core intuitions

Everything is a dot product

A linear model’s prediction is w·x. Cosine similarity is a normalized dot product. Attention scores are dot products. If dot products feel abstract, everything downstream feels abstract.

MLE is the loss factory

Pick a probability model for your data, write the likelihood of what you observed, take the negative log — and you have derived a loss function:

  • Gaussian noise around a linear prediction → squared error.
  • Bernoulli outcomes with a sigmoid probability → log-loss / cross-entropy.
  • Laplace noise → absolute error.

This one insight answers a whole family of “why this loss?” questions across Modules 06, 07, 11 and 17.

Regularization is a prior

Maximizing the posterior (MAP) rather than the likelihood adds a term encoding your prior belief about the parameters. A Gaussian prior on the weights gives L2 / ridge; a Laplace prior gives L1 / lasso. Regularization is not a hack — it is Bayesian inference in disguise.

Covariance is the object PCA diagonalizes

PCA takes the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix: the directions of maximum variance. They are orthogonal because the covariance matrix is symmetric — a consequence, not an imposed constraint.

4 · Hands-on drill

In pure numpy, no sklearn:

  1. Implement mean, variance and the covariance matrix from scratch; verify against numpy.
  2. Sample 10,000 means of 30 draws from an exponential distribution and plot the histogram. Watch the CLT happen.
  3. Implement a bootstrap 95% confidence interval for the median of a skewed sample.
  4. On paper: starting from a Gaussian likelihood, show that maximizing it is equivalent to minimizing squared error. Then do the same for Bernoulli → log-loss. These two derivations appear directly in Modules 06 and 07.
⚠️
Gotchas that fail candidates
  • Misinterpreting a p-value. It is not the probability the null hypothesis is true.
  • Misinterpreting a confidence interval. A 95% CI does not mean “95% probability the parameter is in this interval”.
  • Confusing the CLT with “data becomes normal”. The CLT is about the distribution of the sample mean.
  • Saying zero correlation implies independence. Only the converse holds.
  • Not being able to state what a singular XᵀX means — perfectly collinear features, or more features than samples.

5 · Interview questions

6 questions
01Explain maximum likelihood estimation, and derive a loss function with it.MLEderivationloss＋
Strong answer

MLE chooses the parameters that make the observed data most probable under an assumed model. Write the likelihood L(θ) = ∏ p(xᵢ|θ), take the log to turn the product into a sum — same argmax, numerically stable — and maximize, equivalently minimizing the negative log-likelihood.

Derivation. Assume yᵢ = w·xᵢ + εᵢ with εᵢ ~ N(0, σ²). Then p(yᵢ|xᵢ,w) = (1/√(2πσ²))·exp(-(yᵢ - w·xᵢ)²/(2σ²)). The log-likelihood is -n/2·log(2πσ²) - (1/2σ²)·Σ(yᵢ - w·xᵢ)². The first term is constant in w, so maximizing the log-likelihood is exactly minimizing Σ(yᵢ - w·xᵢ)² — mean squared error.

So MSE is not an arbitrary choice; it is the MLE under Gaussian noise.

30-second version

MLE picks parameters maximizing the probability of the observed data. Assume Gaussian noise on a linear prediction and the negative log-likelihood reduces exactly to squared error.

What’s being tested

Whether you understand that loss functions come from probabilistic assumptions. This is the single most reusable derivation in ML interviews.

Likely follow-up: “So what does using MSE assume about your errors?” — additive, zero-mean, constant-variance, Gaussian. With heavy tails, MSE over-weights outliers and MAE (the Laplace-noise MLE) or Huber is more appropriate.
02What does a p-value of 0.03 mean?statisticsinferencetrap＋
Strong answer

If the null hypothesis were true, there is a 3% probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is a statement about data given a hypothesis — not about the probability of the hypothesis given the data, and not a measure of effect size. A p-value of 0.03 on a very large sample can accompany a business-irrelevant effect.

30-second version

The probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true. Not the probability the null is true, and not an effect size.

What’s being tested

The most common statistical misconception in the industry. Getting it right is a strong positive signal.

Likely follow-up: “You ran 20 independent tests and one came back at p = 0.04. What do you conclude?” — essentially nothing; at α = 0.05 you expect about one false positive in 20 by construction. Apply a multiple-comparison correction, or pre-register a single primary metric.
03A disease affects 1 in 1,000. A test has 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity. You test positive — what is the probability you have it?Bayesbase ratederivation＋
Strong answer

P(D|+) = P(+|D)P(D) / P(+). Prior P(D) = 0.001 and P(+|D) = 0.99, so the true-positive mass is 0.001 × 0.99 = 0.00099. The false-positive rate is 1 - 0.95 = 0.05, so the false-positive mass is 0.999 × 0.05 = 0.04995.

P(D|+) = 0.00099 / (0.00099 + 0.04995) ≈ 0.0194 — about 1.9%.

Despite a “99% accurate” test, a positive result means under a 2% chance of disease, because the prior is so small that false positives from the huge healthy population swamp the true positives.

30-second version

About 1.9%. The base rate is so low that false positives from the large healthy population dominate the true positives.

What’s being tested

Base-rate reasoning. This is exactly the arithmetic behind why precision collapses on imbalanced problems in Module 12 — same computation, different vocabulary.

Likely follow-up: “Connect this to a fraud model.” — precision on a rare positive class is bounded by the base rate, so a model with excellent recall and a plausible false-positive rate can still produce a majority-false-positive alert queue.
04Does zero correlation imply independence?correlationindependencetrap＋
Strong answer

No. Independence implies zero correlation, but not the reverse. Pearson correlation measures only linear association: take x ~ Uniform(-1,1) and y = x² — perfectly dependent, correlation ≈ 0. The exception is the joint Gaussian case, where zero correlation does imply independence. The practical consequence is that screening features by correlation-with-target silently discards non-linear predictors.

30-second version

No — only the converse holds. Pearson correlation sees linear association only; y = x² has correlation near zero while being perfectly dependent.

What’s being tested

Whether you know the limits of your default diagnostic tool, and the feature-selection consequence.

Likely follow-up: “How would you detect non-linear dependence?” — mutual information, Spearman rank correlation for monotone relationships, distance correlation, or simply fit a shallow tree and inspect its gain.
05What is the bias of an estimator, and give an example of a biased one.estimatorbiasvariance＋
Strong answer

Bias is E[θ̂] - θ: the systematic error of the estimation procedure across repeated samples. The maximum-likelihood variance estimator dividing by n is biased downward; dividing by n-1 (Bessel’s correction) is unbiased, because one degree of freedom is consumed estimating the mean. Crucially, unbiasedness is not automatically desirable — ridge regression is deliberately biased in exchange for a large variance reduction, and often achieves lower total error.

30-second version

The systematic error of the procedure across repeated samples. Dividing variance by n is biased low; n−1 corrects it. But biased can be better — that is exactly ridge’s bargain.

What’s being tested

Setup for the bias–variance decomposition in Module 05. The “biased can be better” point is what distinguishes a strong answer.

06Explain Simpson’s paradox with a case where it would change your decision.confoundingexperimentation＋
Strong answer

A trend present in every subgroup can reverse when the subgroups are aggregated, because group membership confounds the comparison. Classic case: treatment A appears better than B overall, but B is better within both mild and severe cohorts — because B was disproportionately given to severe cases.

Decision impact: an aggregate A/B readout showing variant B winning can be driven entirely by a shift in traffic mix — for example more mobile users landing in one arm — so you must check per-segment results and randomization balance before shipping.

30-second version

A trend in every subgroup can reverse on aggregation when group membership confounds the comparison. So an aggregate A/B win can be pure traffic-mix artefact.

What’s being tested

Confounding awareness — the core skill in any experimentation-adjacent role.

Likely follow-up: “How do you protect against it?” — randomize properly, pre-register segments, check covariate balance across arms, and use stratified or CUPED-style variance reduction.
